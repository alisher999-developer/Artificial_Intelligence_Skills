# Deep Research Skill

## Core Principles
1. **Question first.** A vague question produces a vague report. Pin down the decision the research supports.
2. **Breadth, then depth.** Map the landscape, then drill into what matters.
3. **Triangulate.** No important claim rests on a single source. Independent corroboration beats repetition.
4. **Primary over secondary.** Go to the original data, filing, paper, statute, or dataset.
5. **Recency matters.** Anything about the "current state" (prices, rules, leaders, versions, rankings) must be looked up, not recalled.
6. **Be honest about uncertainty.** Separate established fact, credible estimate, and speculation. Report gaps and disagreements.
7. **Synthesize, don't summarize.** The value is the insight across sources, not a list of what each said.

## Phase 1 - Scope
Write down (briefly):
- **Decision / purpose:** what will the reader do with this?
- **Audience & depth:** executive skim, analyst, technical expert?
- **Key questions:** 3-7 specific sub-questions.
- **Boundaries:** geography, time window, industry, definitions.
- **Success criteria:** what would make the answer complete?
- **Assumptions:** state them; ask only if a wrong guess is expensive.

## Phase 2 - Plan the Search
- Decompose the main question into sub-questions; one search thread per sub-question.
- List source types per thread: academic papers, government/statistical agencies, regulatory filings (SEC, EDGAR), company reports, industry bodies, reputable news, expert blogs, datasets, patents, court records, primary interviews/transcripts.
- Prepare query variants: synonyms, jargon, the opposing view, "criticism of X", "X limitations", "X vs Y", "X statistics 2026", region/language variants.
- Search for the strongest counter-argument to your emerging thesis on purpose.

## Phase 3 - Gather
- Start broad (short 1-4 word queries), then narrow with specifics (names, numbers, dates).
- Open the full source - snippets mislead. Read methodology, footnotes, sample size, date, funding.
- Record for every finding: **claim - source - date - source type - confidence - quote location**.
- Follow citations backward (what does this study cite?) and forward (who cites it? has it been challenged or retracted?).
- When a query misses, reformulate with different terms; don't repeat the same phrasing.
- Scale effort to complexity: single fact = 1 search; medium task = 3-8; deep question = 10-20+.

## Source Quality Tiers
| Tier | Examples | Use |
|---|---|---|
| 1 - Primary / authoritative | Raw datasets, filings, statutes, peer-reviewed papers, official statistics, original documents | Anchor claims |
| 2 - Credible secondary | Established news orgs, reputable think tanks, industry analysts, textbooks, review articles | Context, corroboration |
| 3 - Weak / needs corroboration | Blogs, press releases, vendor marketing, social posts, forums, aggregators | Leads only, never sole support |
| 4 - Unreliable | Anonymous, anonymous-funded, SEO content farms, AI-generated spam | Do not cite |

Always ask: Who made this? Why? What do they gain? How do they know? When was it published? Is it independent?

## Phase 4 - Evaluate & Reconcile
- **Conflicts:** explain *why* sources differ (definition, date, sample, methodology, incentives) rather than picking one silently.
- **Numbers:** check units, currency, year, denominator, definition, and whether it is a forecast or an actual.
- **Bias:** note funding, ideology, commercial interest; weigh accordingly.
- **Staleness:** flag data older than the field's pace of change.
- **Circular sourcing:** ten articles quoting one press release is one source.
- **Confidence labels:** High (multiple independent primary sources agree) / Medium (credible but limited) / Low (single source, indirect, or contested).

## Phase 5 - Synthesize
1. Find patterns, causal drivers, trade-offs, and second-order effects.
2. Build the answer to the original decision question: options, evidence for/against, recommendation (if asked).
3. Quantify where possible: ranges, base rates, sensitivities.
4. Surface what is *unknown* and what evidence would change the conclusion.
5. Red-team your own conclusion: what is the strongest objection?

## Phase 6 - Report Structure
1. **Title & one-line answer / bottom line up front**
2. **Executive summary** (5-8 bullets or a tight paragraph): findings, implications, recommendation, confidence
3. **Scope & method** (2-4 lines: what was searched, time window, limitations)
4. **Findings** organized by sub-question or theme, each with evidence and inline citations
5. **Analysis / implications** - what it means for the decision
6. **Risks, uncertainties, open questions**
7. **Recommendations / next steps** (if applicable)
8. **Sources** - numbered list: Title, Publisher, Date, URL

Formatting: headers for navigation, tables for comparisons, short paragraphs, bold the key numbers. Visuals when they clarify (charts for trends, 2x2 for positioning, timelines for sequences).

## Citation & Integrity Rules
- Cite every non-obvious factual claim; link to the specific page.
- Paraphrase; keep direct quotes short and attributed. Never reproduce long copyrighted passages.
- Never fabricate a source, quote, statistic, or URL. If you cannot verify it, say so.
- Distinguish what the source says from your inference.
- Say "I could not find..." rather than filling gaps from memory.

## When to Stop
Stop when: each key sub-question has an answer backed by at least two independent credible sources (or you've documented why it can't); new searches return only repeats; the counter-arguments have been addressed; and the remaining uncertainty is stated.

## Research Types - Quick Playbooks
- **Market / competitor:** size & growth (TAM/SAM/SOM with method), segments, players & share, pricing, business models, trends, regulation, risks. Triangulate top-down and bottom-up.
- **Technology comparison:** criteria matrix, benchmarks (with methodology), maturity, ecosystem, cost, lock-in, security, migration effort, version/date of each claim.
- **Literature review:** define search terms and inclusion criteria, map the field, group by theme/method, weigh study quality, note consensus vs debate and gaps.
- **Policy / legal:** primary text first (statute, regulation, ruling), jurisdiction, effective dates, interpretation, enforcement, pending changes. Not legal advice.
- **Due diligence on a person/company:** public records, filings, reputable press, litigation, regulatory actions; corroborate negatives carefully; respect privacy; no targeting of private individuals.

## Quality Checklist
- [ ] Question and decision clearly stated
- [ ] Multiple independent, high-quality sources per key claim
- [ ] Current information verified by search, with dates
- [ ] Contradictions explained, not hidden
- [ ] Confidence levels and limitations stated
- [ ] Opposing views represented fairly
- [ ] Numbers checked (units, year, definition)
- [ ] Every claim traceable to a source
- [ ] Insight and implications - not just a summary
- [ ] Clear structure; bottom line up front
