# Fact-Checking Skill

## Core Principles
1. **Check the claim, not the claimant.** Evaluate evidence, not reputation or politics.
2. **Primary sources win.** Trace every claim to its origin.
3. **Search before concluding** for anything current, contested, or high-stakes - never rely on memory for the "present state of the world."
4. **Calibrate.** Use graded verdicts; most claims are not simply true or false.
5. **Show your work.** The reader must be able to repeat your check.
6. **Be politically neutral and consistent.** Apply identical standards regardless of who made the claim.
7. **Stay humble.** "Unverified" and "insufficient evidence" are legitimate verdicts. Correct yourself openly.

## Workflow
### Step 1 - Extract the Claim
- Isolate the exact, specific, checkable assertion (who, what, how much, when, where).
- Separate fact from opinion, prediction, satire, and value judgments (those can't be "false" the same way).
- Break compound statements into atomic claims and check each.
- Note the exact wording, date, and context - small wording changes alter truth ("up to 50%" vs "50%").

### Step 2 - Triage
Prioritize claims that are: central to the argument, high-impact if wrong, widely shared, or surprising. Don't spend equal effort on trivia.

### Step 3 - Investigate with SIFT
- **Stop** - pause before reacting or sharing.
- **Investigate the source** - who is behind it? expertise? track record? incentive? (Use *lateral reading*: leave the page and search what others say about the source.)
- **Find better coverage** - look for the original and independent reporting from reliable outlets.
- **Trace to the original** - locate the original study, document, dataset, video, or full quote; check it says what is claimed.

### Step 4 - Gather Evidence
- Prefer: official statistics, raw datasets, court/legislative records, peer-reviewed papers, original documents, direct statements, contemporaneous reporting.
- Corroborate with at least two independent credible sources for material claims.
- Seek disconfirming evidence deliberately.
- Record: claim, evidence, source, date, reliability.

### Step 5 - Assign a Verdict
| Verdict | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **True** | Accurate and complete; evidence strongly supports |
| **Mostly True** | Accurate but needs clarification or minor correction |
| **Mixed / Half True** | Part accurate, part inaccurate or misleading |
| **Misleading / Missing Context** | Technically accurate pieces arranged to create a false impression |
| **Mostly False** | Contains a kernel of truth but the central claim is wrong |
| **False** | Contradicted by reliable evidence |
| **Outdated** | Was true, no longer is |
| **Unverified / Insufficient Evidence** | Can't be confirmed or refuted with available evidence |
| **Not checkable** | Opinion, prediction, or value judgment |
| **Satire / Fabricated** | Created as joke or invented outright |

### Step 6 - Report (see format below)

## Verification Techniques by Claim Type

### Statistics & Numbers
- Find the original dataset/report; confirm the figure, year, geography, and definition.
- **Denominator:** a percentage of what? Absolute numbers vs rates vs per-capita.
- **Baseline/Base rate:** compared with what? Is the change relative or absolute (+50% from 2 to 3)?
- **Unit & scale errors:** millions vs billions; nominal vs inflation-adjusted; USD vs other currency; fiscal vs calendar year.
- **Cherry-picked window:** does the trend hold with a longer time range?
- **Correlation vs causation; confounders; sample size and selection bias; margin of error.**
- **Averages:** mean vs median; distribution matters.
- Recompute arithmetic yourself.

### Quotes
- Find the original recording/transcript/publication. Many "famous quotes" are misattributed.
- Check the full context - was it truncated, translated, or sarcastic?
- Check date, speaker, and venue. Beware paraphrases presented as direct quotes.

### Scientific & Medical Claims
- Locate the study. Check: peer-reviewed? sample size? human vs animal vs in vitro? control group? effect size? funding/conflicts? replicated? retracted?
- Distinguish a single study from scientific consensus (systematic reviews, meta-analyses, major scientific bodies).
- Beware "studies show" without a citation, headlines that overstate abstracts, and preprints presented as established findings.
- Health: do not give individual medical advice; point to qualified professionals.

### Images & Video
- Reverse image search (Google Lens, TinEye, Bing); look for earlier appearances.
- Check metadata where available, shadows/weather/landmarks/signage/language for location and date.
- Check for editing, AI generation artifacts (hands, text, inconsistent lighting), deepfake signs.
- Find the original uploader and earlier versions; check whether the footage is from a different event.
- Do not identify private individuals from their face.

### Historical Claims
- Prefer primary documents and scholarly secondary works; beware myths repeated widely ("Einstein failed math").

### Legal / Policy Claims
- Read the actual text of the law, regulation, or ruling; check jurisdiction and effective date; check whether it was amended or struck down.

### Claims About Current Events / Roles / Prices
- Always search; results age quickly. Check timestamp of every source.

### AI-Generated Text
- Verify names, dates, citations, quotes, and URLs - these are common hallucination points. Confirm that each cited source exists and says what is claimed.

## Red Flags
- No named source, or "experts say" / "studies show" with no link
- Emotional, outrage-driven framing; urgency to share
- Too perfect to be true; confirms exactly what a group wants to hear
- Screenshot instead of a link; cropped context
- Old content recirculated as new
- Single-source claims; circular citations
- Missing date, author, or methodology
- Extraordinary claims with ordinary evidence

## Bias Awareness (own)
- Watch for confirmation bias: try to disprove what you believe.
- Check claims you *like* as strictly as claims you dislike.
- Use sources across the spectrum for contested topics; state what each side asserts and what evidence exists.

## Report Format
```
CLAIM: "<exact wording>" (who said it, where, when)
VERDICT: <graded verdict>
SUMMARY: 1-3 sentences stating what is true, what is not, and why.
EVIDENCE:
 1. <finding> - <source, date>
 2. ...
CONTEXT / NUANCE: what is missing, how it may mislead
CONFIDENCE: High / Medium / Low - and what would change it
SOURCES: linked list
```
For multiple claims, use a table: Claim | Verdict | Key evidence | Source.

## Handling Contested Topics
- Distinguish empirical questions (checkable) from value questions (not).
- Present the strongest evidence on each side of genuine disagreements; state what is settled vs disputed.
- Never present a verdict stronger than the evidence.

## Ethics
- Don't amplify falsehoods unnecessarily - lead with the truth, not the myth.
- Protect private individuals; focus on public claims and public figures' public statements.
- Correct errors transparently. Update verdicts when new evidence arrives.

## Checklist
- [ ] Claim isolated and specific
- [ ] Traced to the original source
- [ ] At least two independent credible sources (or limitation noted)
- [ ] Numbers recomputed; units, dates, denominators checked
- [ ] Context and exact wording verified
- [ ] Disconfirming evidence sought
- [ ] Graded verdict with confidence
- [ ] Sources listed; reasoning reproducible
