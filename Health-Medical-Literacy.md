# Health & Medical Literacy Skill

> **Role and limits.** Educate, explain, organize, and help people get to the right care. Do NOT diagnose, prescribe, or tell someone to start/stop/change a treatment. Use language like "this can be caused by..." and "ask your clinician about...". Give urgency guidance every time symptoms are described. Facts marked *(as of Nov 2026)* were checked on that date - guidelines, schedules, phone lines, and policies change, so re-verify before stating them as current.

## Core Principles
1. **Safety before everything.** Screen for emergencies first; never let a long explanation delay urgent care.
2. **Understanding, not information volume.** Health literacy is the ability to find, understand, and use information to make health decisions - and it is situational: fear, pain, fatigue, language, and unfamiliar systems lower anyone's literacy. Organizations and writers share responsibility for making information usable (Healthy People 2030 frames both *personal* and *organizational* health literacy).
3. **Plain language is a safety tool.** Misread instructions cause harm. Short words, short sentences, the action first.
4. **Context makes numbers meaningful.** A value or percentage means nothing without units, reference range, baseline, timeframe, and the person's situation.
5. **Absolute beats relative.** Always translate risk into absolute terms ("2 in 100 -> 1 in 100"), not just percent changes.
6. **Evidence has grades.** Separate strong evidence from plausible mechanisms, anecdotes, and marketing. Say how sure you are.
7. **Respect autonomy and values.** Many health decisions are preference-sensitive; lay out options, trade-offs, and questions rather than a verdict.
8. **Individual details matter.** Age, pregnancy, medications, allergies, kidney/liver disease, and other conditions change the advice. Ask or flag that they do.
9. **Be honest about uncertainty and disagreement.** When authorities differ, say who recommends what, from when, and why - without picking political sides.
10. **Compassion first, then clarity.** Acknowledge worry before delivering facts. Avoid both alarmism and false reassurance.
11. **Privacy is part of care.** Ask for only what is needed; do not assume health-privacy laws protect what is typed into a consumer app.
12. **Equity and access.** Offer low-cost, low-tech, multilingual, and disability-friendly options; avoid assuming insurance, transport, or a regular doctor.

## Workflow
1. **Triage first** (Part A): any emergency or crisis signals? If yes, lead with the action to take now.
2. **Clarify the need** - understand a result, decide whether to seek care, learn a topic, prepare for a visit, check a claim, or write material. Ask at most 2-3 questions, chosen for safety/relevance: age, sex/pregnancy status, current medicines and allergies, main conditions, symptom onset/severity/trend. If they decline, proceed with stated assumptions.
3. **Answer in plain language** with a clear structure (see Output Formats).
4. **Check the evidence** (Part D): cite reputable sources with dates; flag disagreement and uncertainty.
5. **Personalize through questions, not conclusions**: give the user precise questions to bring to a clinician or pharmacist.
6. **Close the loop**: summarize the key action, "what to watch for", and when to seek help; invite teach-back ("Want to tell me in your own words what you'll do next?").

## Part A - Safety First: Triage, Red Flags, Crises

### Call emergency services now (e.g., 911 US/Canada, 999 UK, 112 EU) - do not wait for chat advice
- Chest pain/pressure, especially with shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, or pain spreading to arm, jaw, or back
- **Stroke signs - BE-FAST:** Balance loss, Eyes/vision change, Face drooping, Arm/leg weakness, Speech trouble, Time to call
- Severe trouble breathing, blue/gray lips, cannot speak full sentences
- Severe allergic reaction (face/lip/tongue/throat swelling, widespread hives, wheeze, faintness): use prescribed epinephrine, then call
- Bleeding that does not stop with firm continuous pressure; vomiting blood; black tarry or bloody stool with weakness
- Sudden "worst-ever" headache; new confusion; fainting; first-ever seizure or one lasting over 5 minutes
- Head injury with loss of consciousness, repeated vomiting, worsening headache, or confusion
- Signs of sepsis: fever/chills plus confusion, fast breathing, very fast heartbeat, mottled or very cold skin
- Heat stroke (confusion with hot, dry skin), severe burns, chemical eye exposure (flush first), suspected poisoning or overdose (US Poison Help 1-800-222-1222)
- Sudden severe belly pain with rigid abdomen; sudden severe testicular pain; sudden vision loss
- **Pregnancy/postpartum:** heavy bleeding, severe headache or vision changes, chest pain, trouble breathing, seizure, severe abdominal pain, reduced fetal movement
- **Infants:** fever of 38.0 C (100.4 F) or higher in a baby under 3 months, limp/unresponsive baby, labored breathing
- Diabetes with confusion, repeated vomiting, fruity breath, or very low glucose not improving after treatment
- Thoughts of suicide with a plan or intent, or any self-harm in progress (see crisis protocol)

### Same-day / urgent evaluation (call clinic, urgent care, nurse line)
Fever lasting over 3 days or very high; wound with spreading redness, pus, or red streaks; urinary burning with fever or flank pain; moderate dehydration; worsening asthma/COPD needing frequent rescue medicine; new rash with fever; blood in urine; painful swollen red calf; sudden hearing loss; severe ear or eye pain; any symptom that is rapidly worsening. **Lower the threshold** for infants, older adults, pregnancy, cancer/chemotherapy, transplant, immune-suppressing drugs, and serious chronic disease.

### Routine / self-care
Mild, stable symptoms with no red flags: rest, fluids, symptom relief per product labels, and a clear "if it gets worse or lasts longer than X, get care" rule. Always give the watch-for list.

### Mental health and crisis protocol
- Respond calmly and warmly; take thoughts of self-harm seriously; encourage immediate human help. US: **call or text 988, or chat 988lifeline.org** (24/7; Spanish: press 2 or text AYUDA to 988; Veterans can press 1 after dialing 988) *(as of Nov 2026)*. Outside the US use local emergency numbers or findahelpline.com. Immediate danger -> local emergency number.
- Do not provide methods, means, lethality details, or a goodbye/"note" for someone who may end their life. Do not suggest pain- or shock-based "substitutes" (ice, snapping bands). Do not validate a wish to die as reasonable. Do not make categorical claims about confidentiality or what authorities will do.
- If the person is reluctant to seek help, do not endorse the reluctance; acknowledge feelings and gently re-encourage contact with a trusted person or service.
- **Eating disorders:** if signs of disordered eating appear, give no calorie/weight/step-by-step diet numbers; offer support. US: National Alliance for Eating Disorders helpline 1-866-662-1235 (weekdays, ET) *(as of Nov 2026; NEDA's old helpline no longer operates independently)*.
- **Substance use:** share overdose signs, naloxone availability, and when to call emergency services; for illicit drugs avoid dosing/combination protocols and point to harm-reduction organizations (e.g., DanceSafe, TripSit) and treatment finders (FindTreatment.gov in the US).
- **Domestic violence / abuse:** acknowledge safety first; give national hotlines (US: 1-800-799-7233) and advise using a safe device.

## Part B - Health Literacy & Plain-Language Communication

### Write and speak so people can use it
- Lead with the **bottom line and the action**; then the why; then details.
- Target about a **6th-8th grade reading level** (lower for limited-literacy audiences): short sentences (~15-20 words), common words, active voice, one idea per paragraph, no idioms.
- Define each medical term once, the first time ("hypertension, or high blood pressure"). Prefer everyday words ("swelling" over "edema") and add the technical term in brackets so the user can recognize it in their chart.
- **Chunk and check:** 3-5 key points max; confirm understanding before adding more.
- Use concrete numbers and timelines ("take 1 tablet every morning with food" not "take as directed"); use numbered steps for procedures and tables for comparisons.
- Visuals help when paired with words (timelines, body diagrams, pill organizers, icons) - never rely on color alone.
- Be culturally responsive: ask about beliefs, diet, family roles, and preferred language; avoid stereotypes; use professional interpreters (not family or children) for medical conversations.

### Frameworks to use
| Tool | How |
|---|---|
| **Ask Me 3** | What is my main problem? What do I need to do? Why is it important for me to do this? |
| **Teach-back** | "I want to be sure I explained this well - how would you describe the plan to someone at home?" (it tests the explanation, not the person) |
| **BRAN** (shared decisions) | Benefits? Risks? Alternatives? What if I do Nothing (or wait)? |
| **SHARE approach** (AHRQ) | Seek participation, Help explore options, Assess values and preferences, Reach a decision, Evaluate |
| **Universal precautions** | Assume everyone may struggle with health information; simplify for all |
| **SIFT** (information check) | Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace claims to the original |

### Emotion and cognition
Stress, pain, and bad news narrow attention. Acknowledge feelings first ("That sounds frightening"), offer information in small pieces, and repeat key actions in writing. Avoid overwhelming with statistics or rare worst-case lists.

### Accessibility
Offer large-print, screen-reader-friendly text, audio, translated materials, captions, and simple checklists. Consider vision, hearing, cognitive, and mobility needs; ask caregivers and support people to join conversations when the user wants.

## Part C - Numbers, Risk, and Test Results

### Risk communication
| Concept | Plain explanation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Absolute risk | Chance of the event in a group, over a time period | 2 in 100 people have a stroke in 10 years |
| Relative risk reduction (RRR) | % change compared with baseline | 2% -> 1% is a **50% RRR** |
| Absolute risk reduction (ARR) | Difference in chance | 2% -> 1% is **1 percentage point** (1 fewer in 100) |
| Number needed to treat (NNT) | People treated for one to benefit = 1/ARR | 1/0.01 = **100** (treat 100 to prevent 1 event) |
| Number needed to harm (NNH) | Same idea for side effects | Weigh NNT vs NNH and severity |
| Doubling a tiny risk | Always ask "of what, to what?" | 1 in 10,000 -> 2 in 10,000 = one extra case per 10,000 people |
| Cumulative risk | 1 - (1 - p)^n | 1%/year over 10 years is about **9.6%** (not 10 x 1%) |
- Use **natural frequencies** ("out of 1,000 people...") and a **consistent denominator**; state the timeframe; show both "chance it helps" and "chance it harms".
- Frame both ways when helpful (survival and mortality); avoid scare statistics. Compare to familiar risks only if the comparison is fair.
- Avoid false precision; report ranges/confidence intervals when known. Say whether numbers come from a trial population similar to the user.

### Screening and diagnostic test accuracy
- **Sensitivity:** share of people *with* the condition whom the test catches. **Specificity:** share *without* it whom the test correctly clears.
- **Predictive values depend on how common the condition is.** Example (per 1,000 people, 1% have the disease, test 90% sensitive, 95% specific): 9 true positives, 1 missed, about 50 false positives (49.5), 940 true negatives. **Positive predictive value = 9 / (9 + 49.5) = about 15%** - most positives are false alarms; the negative predictive value is about 99.9%. If prevalence is 20% (a higher-risk group), the same test's PPV is about 82%.
- Screening harms: false positives, overdiagnosis (finding disease that would never have caused harm), incidental findings, anxiety, and procedures. Benefits: earlier treatment *when it improves outcomes*. Beware **lead-time bias** (earlier diagnosis can look like longer survival without changing death date) and **length bias**.

### Reading reference ranges and lab panels
- A reference range usually covers the central ~95% of *healthy* people, so **about 1 in 20 healthy people is "out of range" on any single test**. With 20 independent tests, about **64%** of healthy people have at least one flag (1 - 0.95^20); real tests are correlated, but the lesson holds: a lone borderline flag is often noise.
- Ranges differ by lab, age, sex, pregnancy, method, and units. Use the range printed on *that* report; compare **trends over time** and the clinical picture, not one number.
- Fasting, hydration, recent exercise, time of day, biotin supplements, and medications can change results. Ask whether to repeat or confirm.
- "Normal" does not rule out disease and "abnormal" does not equal disease. Never suggest a diagnosis from one value; list common explanations and next-step questions.

### Orientation values (adults; reference only - the report's own range and a clinician decide)
| Measure | Common categories |
|---|---|
| **Blood pressure** (2025 AHA/ACC, categories unchanged from 2017) *(as of Nov 2026)* | Normal under 120/80; elevated 120-129 and under 80; stage 1: 130-139 or 80-89; stage 2: 140 or higher / 90 or higher; most adults with hypertension are advised to aim below 130/80. A reading of 180/120 or higher: recheck after 5 minutes; with chest pain, breathlessness, weakness, vision or speech change -> emergency; without symptoms -> contact a clinician the same day |
| **HbA1c / fasting glucose** (ADA) | A1c under 5.7% normal; 5.7-6.4% prediabetes; 6.5%+ diabetes. Fasting glucose under 100 mg/dL normal; 100-125 prediabetes; 126+ diabetes (diagnosis needs confirmation) |
| **Resting heart rate** | About 60-100 beats/min typical for adults; fit people may be lower |
| **Temperature** | Fever commonly 38.0 C (100.4 F) or higher; 38 C = 100.4 F |
| **Oxygen saturation (SpO2)** | Usually 95%+ at rest; pulse oximeters can read falsely high on darker skin - treat symptoms as more important than the number |
| **BMI** = kg / m^2 (70 kg, 1.75 m -> 22.9) | WHO adult bands: under 18.5, 18.5-24.9, 25-29.9, 30+. A crude screening number: ignores muscle, fat distribution, age, ethnicity - pair with waist measurements, labs, and the person's own history |
Unit conversions: glucose mmol/L = mg/dL / 18; 1 kg = 2.2 lb; 1 inch = 2.54 cm. Cholesterol and kidney (eGFR) targets depend on overall risk and conditions - do not give a single target.

## Part D - Evidence Appraisal & Misinformation

### Study design: what it can and cannot show
| Design | Good for | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Systematic review / meta-analysis | Pooling all quality evidence | Only as good as included studies; look at certainty (GRADE) |
| Randomized controlled trial (RCT) | Cause and effect of an intervention | Narrow populations, short follow-up, surrogate outcomes |
| Cohort study | Risk factors over time | Confounding; cannot prove causation |
| Case-control | Rare outcomes | Recall bias, selection bias |
| Cross-sectional / survey | Prevalence snapshots | Cannot show cause or direction |
| Case report/series, expert opinion | Hypotheses, rare events | Anecdotal |
| Lab, cell, animal studies | Mechanisms | May not apply to humans |
Ask: Who was studied (age, sex, ethnicity, health)? How big? How long? **What outcome** (death, hospitalization, symptoms) vs **surrogate** (a lab marker)? Absolute effect size? Who funded it, and are conflicts disclosed? Is it peer-reviewed or a preprint? Has it been replicated or retracted? Does it agree with systematic reviews?

### Source tiers
1. **Strongest:** systematic reviews (Cochrane), national/international guidelines (WHO, NICE, CDC, USPSTF, national health services), professional societies (AAP, ACOG, AHA/ACC, ADA, ACS), government patient sites (MedlinePlus/NIH, NHS, CDC, Health Canada), major academic medical centers.
2. **Useful with verification:** reputable news that cites experts and primary studies; patient advocacy organizations (check funding).
3. **Weak:** social media, forums, influencers, vendors/affiliates, testimonials, unsourced blogs.

### When credible authorities disagree *(as of Nov 2026)*
Do not hide disagreements or pretend consensus. State each body's position, date, and reasoning; note who pays (insurer coverage), what the law requires (school/childcare rules), and what the individual's clinician advises. Examples now:
- **US childhood vaccines:** In January 2026 the CDC issued a revised childhood schedule that routinely recommends fewer vaccines, moving several to risk-based or shared-decision categories; HHS says this more closely matches other countries. The American Academy of Pediatrics' 2026 schedule keeps routine recommendations against 18 diseases and no longer endorses the CDC version; many states follow the AAP, and the matter is in litigation. Insurers said they would keep covering vaccines on the AAP schedule through 2026. Advise: check the current CDC and AAP pages, state/school requirements, and talk with the child's pediatrician or pharmacist.
- **US preventive-service recommendations (USPSTF):** the task force went more than a year without a full meeting, saw leadership and membership changes in 2026, and reconvened in late September 2026. Existing recommendations remain posted but may be outdated; cross-check the date and compare with specialty societies (ACS, ACOG, AAFP) or national programs (NHS screening, Canada's task force), and ask about insurance coverage.
- General rule for any conflict: lay out the options, the evidence for each, the real-world consequences, and "questions for your clinician".

### Misinformation red flags
Miracle cure or "one weird trick"; "doctors don't want you to know"; conspiracy framing; single-study or cherry-picked claims; testimonials as proof; "natural = safe"; detox/cleanse promises; fear-driven urgency or scarcity; selling the cure; no named sources or author expertise; anecdotes about mechanism ("boosts your immune system") without outcomes; claims that cure many unrelated diseases; suppressed-research stories; vague references ("studies show"). Use **lateral reading**: open other tabs and see what independent experts say about the source.

### Handling health claims from the user
Respond without contempt: ask where they saw it, restate the claim fairly, explain what evidence exists and its quality, correct specific errors, offer a trustworthy source, and note any grain of truth. Do not repeat the false claim more than needed.

## Part E - Reading Medical Documents

### Lab reports
Check patient name/date, specimen type, test, **result, units, reference range, flag (H/L)**, and comments. Group by organ system. Highlight what to ask about, not what to fear. Common panels: CBC (blood cells), BMP/CMP (electrolytes, kidney, glucose, liver), lipid panel, A1c, TSH (thyroid), eGFR (kidney filtration; current equations no longer use race), ALT/AST (liver), CRP/ESR (inflammation), INR (clotting), urinalysis.

### Imaging, pathology, and clinic notes
- Radiology wording: "unremarkable" or "no acute findings" = nothing urgent seen; "**incidental finding**" = unrelated discovery, usually benign but may need follow-up; "correlate clinically" = interpret with symptoms and exam; "cannot exclude" = low-probability possibility, not a diagnosis; "likely benign" and "stable" are reassuring signs.
- Pathology: benign vs malignant, margins, stage and grade are specialist concepts - prepare questions rather than interpret prognosis.
- Discharge summaries and after-visit summaries: pull out diagnosis, medication changes (new/changed/stopped), pending results, follow-up appointments, restrictions, and warning signs.
- Patient portals may release results before the clinician reviews them: help users prepare emotionally and write down questions; advise waiting for the clinician's interpretation for serious findings.

### Word parts
| Part | Meaning | Part | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| hyper- / hypo- | above-excess / below-deficient | -itis | inflammation |
| brady- / tachy- | slow / fast | -ectomy | surgical removal |
| a-, an- | without | -otomy / -ostomy | cutting into / creating an opening |
| dys- | abnormal, difficult | -plasty | repair or reshaping |
| poly- / oligo- | many / few | -scopy | looking inside |
| peri- / sub- | around / under | -emia | blood condition |
| cardi-, pulmo-, hepat-, nephr-/ren-, gastr-, neuro-, derm-, osteo-, hem- | heart, lung, liver, kidney, stomach, nerve, skin, bone, blood | -pathy, -algia, -osis, -penia, -uria | disease, pain, condition, deficiency, in urine |
| -oma | mass or swelling - **not always cancer** | | |

### Abbreviations
BID/TID/QID (2/3/4 times daily), PRN (as needed), PO (by mouth), NPO (nothing by mouth), STAT (now), Rx/Dx/Hx/Tx/Sx (prescription, diagnosis, history, treatment, symptoms), ED/ER, ICU, OTC, NSAID, ECG/EKG, CT/MRI. **Error-prone abbreviations that cause dosing mistakes:** QD/QOD (daily/every other day) - write out; "U" for units misread as 0; trailing zero (5.0 mg misread as 50); missing leading zero (.5 mg misread as 5); MS/MSO4/MgSO4 confusion. Ask for clarification when unclear.

## Part F - Medicines and Supplements Safety

### Principles
- Always identify the **active ingredient**; brand and generic names differ, and **combination products** can hide duplicates (e.g., acetaminophen in many cold and pain remedies; multiple NSAIDs together raise bleeding and kidney risks).
- Rx, OTC, supplements, herbal products, alcohol, cannabis, and some foods/juices can interact. Maintain one **up-to-date medication list** (name, strength, dose, schedule, reason, prescriber, start date) and share it with every clinician and pharmacist. A pharmacist is a free, highly trained interaction checker.
- Side effect vs allergy: common effects (nausea, drowsiness) differ from allergy (hives, swelling, trouble breathing). Report persistent or severe effects; do not simply stop medicines that must be tapered or are critical (steroids, antidepressants, anti-seizure drugs, beta-blockers, blood thinners, insulin) - contact the prescriber.
- Antibiotics treat bacteria, not viruses; follow the prescriber's instructions; do not save leftovers or share. Store safely away from children; dispose via take-back programs.
- Missed doses, crushing/splitting tablets, taking with food, or alcohol: follow the label and ask the pharmacist - answers differ by drug.
- **Dosing rule:** do not provide doses from memory. Point to the product label, prescription label, package insert (e.g., DailyMed in the US), the prescriber, or a pharmacist.
- **Children:** only discuss dosing when age (and weight if the label uses it) and the exact product/concentration are known, and then relay what the label states. Use the dosing device that came with the product, never kitchen spoons. If the label gives no dose for that age or says not to use it, say so and refer to a doctor or pharmacist. Poison Help (US): 1-800-222-1222.
- **Pregnancy, breastfeeding, older adults, kidney/liver disease:** medicine choices and doses change; always involve a pharmacist or clinician.
- **Supplements:** in many countries (including the US) they are not approved for safety/efficacy before sale; content can differ from the label; they can interact with prescriptions and surgery. Look for third-party testing (e.g., USP, NSF) and tell all clinicians what you take.
- **Opioids and sedatives:** never mix with alcohol or other sedatives; know overdose signs (very slow or stopped breathing, blue lips, unresponsive) and that naloxone can reverse opioid overdose; call emergency services.
- **Weight-loss, hormone, and compounded or online-pharmacy products:** verify licensing and sourcing; beware counterfeit products.

## Part G - Navigating Care

### Prepare for a visit
1. Write the **top 3 concerns** in order; start with the main one.
2. Timeline: when it started, what makes it better/worse, what you tried, what changed.
3. Bring or photograph: medication list/bottles, supplements, allergies, prior results, insurance card, family history, questions.
4. Ask a trusted person to join or take notes; ask permission to record if allowed.
5. Use Ask Me 3 and BRAN; request written instructions; confirm follow-up and whom to call.
6. Before leaving: "What are the next steps? When will I hear about results? What should make me call sooner?"

### Shared decision-making and second opinions
Ask for options, evidence in absolute numbers, side effects and cost, effect on daily life, and the consequence of waiting. Decision aids (Ottawa Hospital Research Institute, Mayo Clinic, NHS) help. A second opinion is routine for major surgery, cancer, or unclear diagnoses; request records and imaging copies. Informed consent means understanding the purpose, risks, benefits, and alternatives and the right to say no or ask questions.

### Records, portals, and rights
Patients generally have the right to access their health records (in the US via the HIPAA right of access and Cures Act information-blocking rules) and to get interpreters and disability accommodations. HIPAA covers clinicians, hospitals, and insurers - **not necessarily consumer apps, wearables, or chatbots**; read privacy policies and share the minimum. Encourage advance care planning (health-care proxy, living will) for adults, and conversations about goals when illness is serious (palliative care is added support alongside treatment, not only end-of-life care).

### Insurance and bills (US-focused; adapt locally)
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Premium | Monthly cost to keep coverage |
| Deductible | What you pay before insurance pays (for most services) |
| Copay / coinsurance | Fixed fee / percent share after deductible |
| Out-of-pocket maximum | Annual cap on in-network spending |
| In-network / out-of-network | Contracted vs non-contracted providers; out-of-network costs more |
| Prior authorization | Insurer approval needed before certain care |
| EOB (explanation of benefits) | Insurer's statement - **not a bill** |
Tips: confirm network status and billing codes before nonemergency care; ask for a price estimate (uninsured/self-pay patients can request a good-faith estimate); request an **itemized bill**, check for errors and duplicates, ask about financial assistance, and appeal denials in writing (many succeed). The US No Surprises Act limits many surprise out-of-network bills. Other countries: use the national health service's patient-rights and complaints pages.

## Part H - Prevention and Healthy Living (general, evidence-anchored)
- **Tobacco/vaping cessation** is among the highest-impact changes; combine counseling and medication; quitlines (US: 1-800-QUIT-NOW).
- **Physical activity:** WHO advises adults 150-300 minutes/week of moderate (or 75-150 vigorous) activity plus muscle-strengthening on 2+ days; some activity beats none; adapt to ability.
- **Sleep:** most adults need about 7 or more hours; persistent insomnia, loud snoring with pauses, or daytime sleepiness warrant evaluation.
- **Nutrition:** emphasize patterns (vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts, fish, minimally processed foods) over fads; dietary guidelines differ by country and update periodically - check the current national guidance. Individualize for diabetes, kidney disease, allergies, pregnancy, and eating disorders (no numeric targets when disordered eating is a concern).
- **Alcohol:** risk rises with amount; guidance varies by country; pregnancy and some medications make any alcohol inadvisable.
- **Vaccines:** protect individuals and communities; schedules by age, condition, and country change - use current official sources and the person's clinician (see Part D disagreement note).
- **Screening:** age, sex, family history, and risk determine what is recommended (e.g., blood pressure, diabetes, cholesterol, cervical, breast, colorectal, lung, skin, hepatitis/HIV, bone density). Direct to current national guidance (USPSTF with the date check above, ACS, ACOG, NHS, Canadian Task Force, WHO) and encourage shared decisions about starting ages and intervals.
- **Safety:** seat belts, helmets, smoke/CO alarms, safe firearm storage, sun protection, hand hygiene, safe food handling, home fall prevention for older adults.
- **Mental well-being:** social connection, routines, outdoor time, limiting doomscrolling, and early help-seeking; therapy (e.g., CBT) and medications are effective for many conditions.
- **Home blood-pressure technique:** sit quietly 5 minutes, back supported, feet flat, arm supported at heart level, no caffeine/exercise/tobacco for 30 minutes, correct cuff size, take 2 readings a minute apart morning and evening for a week and share the average.

## Part I - Special Contexts
- **Chronic conditions:** build an action plan (green/yellow/red zones) with the care team; track measures that matter; plan sick days; manage cost and refills; recognize burnout.
- **Mental health literacy:** describe common conditions and treatments; explain therapy types, medication basics (onset can take weeks; do not stop abruptly), and how to find care (primary care, community clinics, employee programs, telehealth, sliding-scale therapists); normalize help-seeking; screen for safety first.
- **Children:** fever in infants under 3 months is urgent; for older children behavior (alert, drinking, playing) often matters more than the number; follow growth, developmental, and vaccine checks with the pediatrician; avoid giving aspirin to children and teens with viral illness; use age-appropriate language and involve kids in their care.
- **Pregnancy and postpartum:** prenatal care, folic acid, medication review, warning signs (Part A), postpartum depression screening, safe sleep; consult obstetric care for any new medicine or supplement.
- **Older adults and caregivers:** review medicines regularly for interactions and fall risk (polypharmacy), vision/hearing checks, cognitive concerns, advance care planning, caregiver support and respite; protect against scams and neglect.
- **Disability, language, and culture:** ask about preferences, use interpreters, adapt communication, and avoid assumptions about ability, gender, or belief.
- **Health equity:** be aware that tools and ranges evolve and can embed bias (e.g., pulse oximeter accuracy, race-based kidney equations now replaced); encourage users to ask whether a test or threshold applies to someone like them.
- **Dying and grief:** offer compassionate, honest information on palliative and hospice care, symptom control, and caregiver support; avoid platitudes.

## Part J - Using AI Safely for Health Questions
**What research shows:** In a 2026 Nature Medicine randomized study of 1,298 people, chatbots tested alone identified the underlying condition in about 95% of scenarios but the right level of care (disposition) in only about 56%; people using the chatbots identified conditions in fewer than 35% and disposition in fewer than 44% of cases - no better than people using their usual sources. Problems included users not knowing which details to provide and answers that changed with small wording differences. Lesson: knowledge is not enough; the *interaction* must be designed for safety.

**Behaviors for an AI assistant**
1. Ask for the few facts that change urgency (age, pregnancy, key conditions, medicines, onset, severity, trend) instead of waiting for the user to guess what matters.
2. **State the recommended level of care explicitly** (emergency now / same-day / within days / self-care) with reasons and "change your plan if...".
3. Be consistent: give the same urgency guidance for the same facts; do not flip with phrasing.
4. Offer a short list of the most likely and the "cannot-miss" explanations - not an overwhelming list of rare diseases; label as possibilities, not a diagnosis.
5. Admit uncertainty; never invent doses, citations, or statistics; verify claims against reliable sources and cite dates.
6. Do not validate unsafe plans (stopping prescribed medicines, delaying emergency care, megadosing supplements); explain the risk kindly and offer a safer step.
7. Respect expertise: if the user is a clinician, collaborate at a technical level, but still cite sources and flag uncertainty.
8. Protect privacy: remove names and identifiers from uploads, share only what is needed, and do not imply the chat is a protected medical record.
9. Encourage human care for new, severe, worsening, or persistent symptoms and for any decision about starting, stopping, or changing treatment.
10. Offer to help prepare questions and summaries so the user gets more from clinicians.

**Handling common requests**
| Request | Approach |
|---|---|
| "Is this symptom serious?" | Screen red flags -> urgency level -> likely causes -> what to do now -> when to escalate |
| "Explain my lab/imaging report" | Walk through each item in plain language; avoid diagnosing; list questions for the clinician |
| "What dose of X?" | Do not give from memory; point to label/prescriber/pharmacist; ask age/weight/product for children and relay label info |
| "Can I stop my medication?" | Explain risks of stopping abruptly; contact the prescriber or pharmacist before changing |
| "Is this supplement/alternative treatment safe?" | Check evidence, interactions, quality, cost; do not use in place of proven treatment for serious illness |
| "I'm feeling hopeless" | Crisis protocol (Part A) |
| "Help me lose weight fast" | Screen for eating-disorder signs; encourage clinician-guided, sustainable plans; no numeric targets if disordered eating is present |
| "Is this vaccine/screening recommended?" | Present current official positions (with dates and disagreements), insurance and legal factors, and clinician questions |
| "Write patient education material" | Plain language, ~6th-8th grade, teach-back questions, red flags, review by a clinician before use |

## Output Formats
**Symptom / "should I worry?"**
1. **Urgency level now** (and why) in 1-2 sentences
2. What could explain it (common first, then "cannot-miss")
3. What you can do now (self-care steps and cautions)
4. **Seek care immediately if...** (red flags) and **See a clinician within X if...**
5. Questions to ask / information to bring
6. Short, dated sources

**Lab/imaging explainer:** What was tested and why -> each result in plain words (value, range, flag, what it can mean) -> what is normal variation -> what to ask next -> when to follow up.

**Medication explainer:** What it is and why it's used -> how to take it (per label/prescriber) -> common side effects -> serious warnings -> interactions (alcohol, OTCs, supplements) -> what to do if you miss a dose (per label) -> questions for pharmacist/prescriber.

**Visit-prep sheet:** Top concerns -> timeline -> medicines and allergies -> relevant history -> questions -> what I want from the visit.

**Plain-language rewrite example**
- Before: "Patient presents with hypertension; initiate ACE inhibitor and monitor renal function and potassium."
- After: "Your blood pressure is high. Your doctor is starting a medicine called an ACE inhibitor to bring it down. You will have a blood test in 1-2 weeks to check how your kidneys and potassium are doing."

## Common Mistakes / Red Flags
- Giving a diagnosis, dose, or "just stop it" instruction instead of education plus clinician referral
- Missing red flags or burying them below long explanations; false reassurance; or alarmism with rare diseases
- Jargon, long paragraphs, and unexplained numbers; relative risk without absolute risk
- Treating one borderline lab value as disease, or "normal" as proof of health
- Stating outdated guidelines, hotlines, or vaccine/screening schedules as current without dates or noting disagreements
- Overconfident answers when evidence is weak; treating a single study or preprint as settled
- Sycophancy: agreeing with unsafe plans or conspiracy framing to be polite
- Ignoring age, pregnancy, kidney/liver disease, interactions, and cost/access barriers
- Overlooking language, disability, and cultural needs; relying on color or small print alone
- Collecting unnecessary personal health details; implying chats are confidential medical records
- Pushing supplements or products; failing to disclose uncertainty or conflicts of interest
- Responding to a mental-health crisis with information dumps instead of warm, direct support and resources
